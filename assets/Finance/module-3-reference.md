# Startup Finance — Module 3, fully authored (owner reference, received 2026-09-04)

**Days 14–20 · Valuing a company with no cash flows**

The module where a PGDM Finance graduate's existing knowledge gets extended rather than repeated. Anchored on Damodaran, who has published more free, high-quality material on exactly this problem than anyone else alive.

---

## Why this module is different for this learner

They already know DCF, comparables, WACC, and enterprise versus equity value. Nothing here re-teaches that.

What they do not have is the answer to: **what do you do when the inputs to a DCF do not exist?** No history, negative earnings, no comparable public company at the same stage, and a survival probability well below one. Their degree taught valuation for companies that have a past. This module is about the ones that do not.

**Do not let this module become revision.** Every day must add something their course did not cover, and where it touches familiar ground the framing must be new.

## Day 14 · Why a textbook DCF fails on a startup
**~80 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *A DCF on a pre-revenue company is a spreadsheet-shaped opinion.*

**Why today:** You can build a DCF. Today is about knowing when it produces a number that means nothing — and being able to say so to a founder who has built one and believes it.

**Today's topics**
1. **What a DCF requires** — a forecastable cash flow, a discount rate that reflects the risk, and a terminal value that is not most of the answer. A startup usually fails all three.
2. **Where each input breaks** — no history to forecast from, no beta to derive a discount rate, and a terminal value that can be 90% or more of the total.
3. **Survival probability** — the input a standard DCF has no place for, and the one that dominates early-stage value.
4. **What a DCF is still good for** — forcing explicit assumptions, testing whether a business could ever be worth the price, and structuring an argument.
5. **The false precision problem** — a number to two decimal places built on a growth assumption someone invented.

**Read & watch**
- **Damodaran — "Valuing Young, Start-up and Growth Companies"**, sections 1–3 · *Why this one: the definitive treatment of exactly this problem, by the person everyone else cites. You read sections 1–2 on day 1 for framing; read them again properly now, and continue to 3.*
- **Damodaran (YouTube) — the young-company valuation lectures** · *Why this one: he works through the estimation problems on screen rather than asserting them. Search his channel for the young/growth company sessions.*
- **Damodaran's blog, Musings on Markets** — a recent post valuing a specific company · *Why this one: watching him do it on a real company, publicly, with his assumptions stated, is worth more than the theory.*
- **A published startup DCF you can find online** · *Why this one: find one, then locate the single assumption carrying the answer. There always is one.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Take any DCF you built during your PGDM. Recalculate what percentage of the total value sits in the terminal value. Then change the terminal growth rate by half a percentage point and record the change in enterprise value. Write two sentences on what that tells you about the reliability of the original number.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why is a beta unavailable for a startup?* → Beta is derived from a share price history against a market. A private company with no traded shares has none, so you must borrow one from comparables and adjust — which imports assumptions rather than removing them.
2. *If terminal value is 85% of your DCF, what have you actually valued?* → Your assumption about a state ten years away, not the forecast you spent a week building. This is the standard failure mode of startup DCFs.
3. *What does a DCF still contribute at seed stage?* → It forces you to state what must be true for the price to make sense. As a reverse-engineering tool it is genuinely useful; as a price-setting tool it is not.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Producing a DCF because it is expected, and defending the output rather than the assumptions. The value of the exercise is the list of things that must be true — that is what you should present.

## Day 15 · Damodaran on young companies
**~90 min · 35 pts**

**Principle:** *You cannot avoid the uncertainty. You can only make it explicit.*

**Why today:** Yesterday established that the standard approach breaks. Today is the most rigorous available answer to what replaces it — and it is not "give up and use a multiple".

**Today's topics**
1. **Estimating revenue from market size down** — start with the addressable market and a defensible share, rather than growing last year's revenue by a rate you chose.
2. **Target margins from a mature comparable** — what does this business look like when it works, and who already looks like that?
3. **Reinvestment and sales-to-capital** — how much capital is required to produce the revenue you have forecast.
4. **A discount rate that changes over time** — high early, converging toward a sector cost of capital as the company matures.
5. **Survival probability as an explicit adjustment** — value the going concern, then weight it by the chance it survives to get there.
6. **Scenario valuation** — three futures with probabilities, rather than one with false confidence.

**Read & watch**
- **Damodaran — "Valuing Young, Start-up and Growth Companies"**, complete · *Why this one: the whole paper today. It is the single most useful free document in this roadmap.*
- **Damodaran's young-company spreadsheet** · *Why this one: open it and take it apart before using it. Understanding how he structures the estimation is the lesson; the formulas are secondary.*
- **Damodaran's data sets — industry margins and sales-to-capital ratios** · *Why this one: this is where your "target margin" comes from. Borrowing a number from a dated report is what you are learning to stop doing.*
- **A Damodaran blog valuation of a recently-listed company** · *Why this one: see the full method applied end to end with every assumption stated.*

**Today's challenge · ~35 min**
Value one Indian startup using Damodaran's structure: market size, target share, target margin from a mature comparable, sales-to-capital, and a survival probability you can justify. State each assumption on a separate line. The number matters less than whether someone could argue with each input individually.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why forecast revenue from market size down rather than growth up?* → Growth rates compound into absurdity. Working down from a market forces you to state a share, which is a claim someone can contest — and that is the point.
2. *Why should the discount rate change over the forecast period?* → A five-year-old company with revenue is less risky than a pre-revenue one. Holding the rate constant either overvalues the early years or undervalues the later ones.
3. *How does survival probability enter the calculation?* → Value the company assuming it succeeds, then multiply by the probability it survives to that point, adding any liquidation value in the failure case. Keeping it separate makes the assumption visible instead of burying it in the discount rate.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Burying failure risk inside an inflated discount rate. A 40% discount rate is a survival assumption in disguise, and disguised assumptions cannot be argued with — which is precisely why people prefer them.

## Day 16 · Revenue and EBITDA multiples
**~70 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *A multiple is a DCF with the assumptions hidden inside it.*

**Why today:** Multiples are how startup valuations are actually discussed in a room. You know how to calculate them; today is about knowing what they are concealing and when they mislead.

**Today's topics**
1. **What a multiple embeds** — growth, margin, risk and reinvestment, compressed into one number. It is not a shortcut past the analysis; it is the analysis, assumed.
2. **Revenue multiples for unprofitable companies** — why they dominate early-stage discussion and what they ignore.
3. **ARR multiples in SaaS** — and why the same multiple means different things at different growth rates and retention levels.
4. **EBITDA multiples** — where they apply and why they are largely irrelevant pre-profitability.
5. **Where the number comes from** — public comparables, transaction comparables, and reported rounds, each with its own bias.
6. **Why reported round multiples are the least reliable** — headline valuations often reflect structure and preference rather than economics.

**Read & watch**
- **Damodaran's data sets — revenue and EBITDA multiples by industry** · *Why this one: free, annual, and defensible. When someone asks where your multiple came from, this is a better answer than a news article.*
- **Damodaran — multiples and relative valuation lectures** · *Why this one: he covers what a multiple implies about growth and risk, which is the part most people skip.*
- **A public SaaS multiples tracker** · *Why this one: see how much multiples move with market conditions. A multiple from eighteen months ago is not a comparable.*
- **An a16z or First Round piece on SaaS valuation multiples** · *Why this one: the practitioner view on why the same ARR multiple means different things at different retention rates.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Take three publicly reported Indian startup rounds in one sector. Calculate the implied revenue multiple for each. Then write two sentences per company explaining why they differ — and one sentence on which of the three numbers you trust least, and why.

**Check yourself**
1. *Two SaaS companies both at 10x ARR. What might justify it?* → Growth rate, net revenue retention, gross margin, market size and capital efficiency. Identical multiples on different fundamentals mean at least one is mispriced.
2. *Why is a headline round valuation unreliable as a comparable?* → It may reflect liquidation preferences, ratchets or structure that make the economic valuation materially lower than the number announced.
3. *When is an EBITDA multiple meaningless?* → When EBITDA is negative or trivially small. A multiple of a number near zero is arithmetic, not valuation.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Treating a multiple as an objective market fact rather than an assumption set. Someone chose the peer group, the period and the metric — and each choice moved the answer.

## Day 17 · Choosing a defensible comparable set
**~70 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *The peer set is where the valuation is really decided.*

**Why today:** You learned comparable company analysis. What a PGDM does not usually teach is that the selection of comparables is the most consequential and least examined step — and in startup work there frequently is no clean comparable at all.

**Today's topics**
1. **What makes a company comparable** — business model, growth, margin, capital intensity and risk. Not sector label.
2. **Why the sector label misleads** — two "fintech" companies can have nothing economically in common.
3. **The startup problem** — no listed company at the same stage, so you compare to mature businesses and adjust, or to reported private rounds and accept bad data.
4. **Adjusting for stage and growth** — and being explicit that you have done so.
5. **Transaction comparables** — what an acquirer paid, and why that includes a control premium and synergies.
6. **How the peer set gets manipulated** — including or excluding two companies can move the median substantially, and nobody checks.

**Read & watch**
- **Damodaran — relative valuation lectures on comparable selection** · *Why this one: he is specific about the adjustment problem, which most treatments skip entirely.*
- **A published equity research report on an Indian listed company** · *Why this one: look at the peer set the analyst chose and ask whether you would have chosen the same. Brokerage reports are widely available free.*
- **Damodaran's data sets — sector groupings** · *Why this one: see how a rigorous source groups companies, and how often the grouping is contestable.*
- **A filed DRHP's peer comparison section** · *Why this one: companies choose their own comparables in a DRHP. Reading one critically is an education in motivated peer selection.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Pick one Indian listed company. Build two defensible peer sets of five companies each — one that makes it look expensive, one that makes it look cheap. Calculate the median multiple for each. Then write one paragraph on which set you would actually defend and why.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why is sector a poor basis for comparability?* → Economics differ within a sector. A payments processor and a lending business are both fintech and have entirely different margin structures, capital needs and risks.
2. *What does a transaction multiple include that a trading multiple does not?* → A control premium and expected synergies. Using transaction comparables to value a minority stake overstates it.
3. *How do you defend a peer set?* → State the selection criteria before showing the result, and show what happens if the two most contestable names are removed. A peer set defended after the fact is not defended.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Selecting comparables, computing a median, and presenting it as market-derived. Every choice in that process was yours, and an experienced reader will test the ones you did not disclose.

## Day 18 · Scorecard, Berkus and the VC method
**~75 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *These are negotiation anchors, not valuations. Know which one you are doing.*

**Why today:** These methods are used constantly in early-stage rounds and appear nowhere in a finance curriculum. They are not rigorous, and understanding *why* is more useful than the methods themselves.

**Today's topics**
1. **The VC method** — work back from an expected exit value and a required return. The most defensible of the group because the logic is explicit.
2. **Scorecard method** — adjust a regional average pre-money by weighted factors: team, market, product, competition.
3. **Berkus method** — assign value to qualitative milestones for pre-revenue companies.
4. **Risk factor summation** — adjust a baseline up or down across a list of risk categories.
5. **What they have in common** — they anchor a negotiation and structure a conversation. None derives value from cash flows.
6. **When each is appropriate**, and how to present one without overstating its rigour.

**Read & watch**
- **A clear written explanation of the VC method with worked arithmetic** · *Why this one: it is the only method in this group with defensible internal logic, so understand it properly before the others.*
- **Damodaran on venture capital valuation approaches** · *Why this one: he treats these methods critically rather than as received wisdom, which is the framing you need.*
- **An angel network's published valuation guidance** · *Why this one: many networks publish their approach. Seeing which method a real investor group uses tells you what you will meet in the room.*
- **A comparison of scorecard and Berkus with examples** · *Why this one: the mechanics are simple; you need one careful read, not four.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Value one pre-revenue startup three ways: the VC method with a stated exit assumption and required return, the scorecard method, and the Berkus method. Then write a paragraph on which you would present to a founder, which to an investment committee, and why they differ.

**Check yourself**
1. *What does the VC method actually require you to assume?* → An exit value, an exit timing, a required return, and expected dilution before exit. Four assumptions, all explicit — which is its main virtue.
2. *Why is the scorecard method not really a valuation?* → It adjusts a regional average, so the answer is anchored to what other people in that region paid. It transmits market conditions rather than deriving value.
3. *When would you use Berkus?* → Pre-revenue, where nothing else has inputs. It is a structured conversation about milestones, and presenting it as more than that is dishonest.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Presenting a scorecard output with the confidence of a DCF. These methods produce a starting point for a negotiation, and describing them accurately is part of advising well.

## Day 19 · Valuation in a negotiation
**~70 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *The number is an output of leverage as much as of analysis.*

**Why today:** Every method so far assumes valuation is analytical. In practice a startup's price is negotiated, and the analysis is one input among several. This is the day the finance training meets the room.

**Today's topics**
1. **What actually moves the number** — competing term sheets, runway remaining, sector heat, the investor's fund cycle, and how badly each side needs the deal.
2. **The founder's position** — dilution, control, and the signalling cost of a valuation that is too high for the next round.
3. **The investor's position** — ownership targets, fund construction, and reserve strategy. Many funds need a minimum percentage, which drives the price more than the model does.
4. **Structure versus price** — how preferences, ratchets and pool timing move economics without changing the headline.
5. **Why an inflated valuation can damage a company** — the down round that follows, and what it does to morale, ESOPs and future terms.
6. **Presenting a valuation you will be challenged on** — lead with assumptions, show the sensitivity, name the weakest input yourself.

**Read & watch**
- **Brad Feld's term sheet series — the valuation and economics posts** · *Why this one: written from the investor side about what is actually negotiated, free on his blog.*
- **YC Library — essays on negotiating a round** · *Why this one: the founder perspective, which you need if you are advising founders.*
- **A First Round Review piece on down rounds** · *Why this one: the consequence of getting this wrong, described by people who lived it.*
- **Damodaran on the difference between value and price** · *Why this one: his value-versus-price distinction is the intellectual frame for the entire day.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Write a one-page valuation recommendation for a founder raising a seed round. Include a range rather than a point, the three assumptions that drive it, what would change your view, and one paragraph on what a valuation 40% above your range would cost them at the next round.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why might an investor push a valuation up rather than down?* → To win a competitive round, or because their fund needs to deploy a certain cheque size at a certain ownership. Price is not always adversarial.
2. *What is the cost of a valuation set too high?* → The next round must clear it. If it cannot, a down round follows — repricing employee options, triggering anti-dilution, and damaging confidence in the company.
3. *Why present a range rather than a number?* → A point estimate invites a debate about precision you do not have. A range with stated drivers moves the conversation to the assumptions, which is where it belongs.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Bringing a model to a negotiation and expecting it to settle the question. The model earns you credibility and frames the discussion. It does not decide the price.

## Day 20 · Sanity-checking someone else's valuation
**~80 min · 40 pts**

**Principle:** *Find the one assumption carrying the whole answer. There always is one.*

**Why today:** The module ends where the job actually is. In advisory work you will assess far more valuations than you build, and the skill of taking one apart quickly is what makes you useful in a meeting.

**Today's topics**
1. **The order to check things in** — terminal value share, revenue growth, margin trajectory, discount rate, share count. Most errors are in the first two.
2. **Reverse-engineering a price** — what growth and margin does this valuation require? Is that plausible for anyone in this sector?
3. **Finding the load-bearing assumption** — change each input by 10% and see which one moves the answer most. That is the assumption to interrogate.
4. **Common manipulations** — a peer set with the two cheapest names removed, a terminal growth rate above the discount rate, revenue recognised aggressively, adjusted EBITDA with too many adjustments.
5. **Checking arithmetic before logic** — hardcodes, broken sum ranges, sign errors and circular references. Errors are more often mechanical than conceptual.
6. **How to raise a problem** — ask what the assumption is based on, rather than announcing that it is wrong.

**Read & watch**
- **Damodaran on reverse DCF** · *Why this one: working backward from a price to the implied assumptions is the single most useful technique in this module.*
- **A public short-seller report on any listed company** · *Why this one: whatever you think of the conclusions, these reports are a masterclass in taking apart someone else's numbers. Read one for method.*
- **The FAST modelling standard, or equivalent free modelling guidance** · *Why this one: knowing what a well-built model looks like tells you where to look in a badly built one.*
- **A filed DRHP's financial section** · *Why this one: real numbers, real disclosures, and real adjustments to interrogate.*

**Today's challenge · ~35 min**
Take a startup valuation you can find — a reported round, a pitch deck, or one you built earlier in this module. Produce a one-page critique: the implied assumptions, the load-bearing input, two things you would want to verify, and the single question you would ask in a meeting.

**Check yourself**
1. *A model shows 60% revenue growth for five straight years. What do you ask?* → What in the market or the business supports that, and who else has ever done it in this sector? Sustained growth at that rate is rare and the burden of proof sits with the model.
2. *Terminal growth is 6% and the discount rate is 9%. Is that acceptable?* → Terminal growth cannot exceed long-run economic growth — the company would eventually exceed the economy. 6% is almost certainly too high, and it inflates terminal value substantially.
3. *You find a hardcoded number in a formula range. Why does it matter beyond the error itself?* → It tells you the model has not been audited, so there are probably more. One hardcode is a reason to check everything.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Rebuilding the model instead of interrogating it. Your value in a meeting is finding the assumption everything rests on in twenty minutes — not producing your own version by Friday.
