# Startup Finance — Module 4, fully authored (owner reference, received 2026-09-05)

**Days 21–27 · Unit economics and traction**

The layer between the pitch narrative and the financial model. Where a founder's story either holds up or does not, and where an adviser earns their fee.

---

## Why this module is the hinge of the roadmap

Module 3 valued the company. Module 5 builds the model. **This module supplies the drivers both of them run on.**

It is also the module with the widest gap between what a finance degree teaches and what startup work requires. A PGDM covers ratio analysis on audited statements. It does not cover cohort retention, net revenue retention, or the fact that every metric in this module has a standard manipulation that founders use and investors expect.

**The organising idea for all seven days:** each metric has a definition, a use, and a way of being gamed. Learn all three or you learn nothing useful.

## Day 21 · CAC, LTV and the ratio everyone quotes wrongly
**~80 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *An LTV:CAC of 3 means nothing without the payback period beside it.*

**Why today:** These two numbers appear in almost every pitch deck and are almost always calculated to flatter. Getting them right is the entry ticket to everything else in this module.

**Today's topics**
1. **What CAC actually includes** — all sales and marketing cost divided by new customers acquired. Blended CAC includes organic customers and is therefore lower and less useful than paid CAC.
2. **Blended versus paid CAC** — and why founders quote blended.
3. **LTV and its assumptions** — gross margin, retention and a discount rate. Change any one and the number moves substantially.
4. **The 3:1 convention** — where it came from and why it is a heuristic rather than a threshold.
5. **Why payback period matters more** — a 3:1 ratio with a 30-month payback is a cash-flow problem regardless of the ratio.
6. **The circularity trap** — LTV built on a retention assumption that came from too little data.

**Read & watch**
- **a16z — the archive on unit economics and the "16 metrics" material** · *Why this one: written by investors about the numbers they are shown and how they are misled. Free, and it names the manipulations directly.*
- **A First Round Review piece on CAC and payback** · *Why this one: practitioner accounts of getting this wrong, which is more instructive than the definitions.*
- **David Skok's writing on SaaS metrics** — his LTV and CAC material · *Why this one: the most widely cited free treatment of the payback question. Long, but the payback section is the part to read carefully.*
- **Damodaran on customer-based valuation** · *Why this one: connects unit economics back to company value, which is the link module 3 needs and most metrics writing ignores.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Take a startup you can find data for. Calculate blended CAC and paid CAC separately. Then calculate LTV under two retention assumptions — one optimistic, one conservative — and produce both ratios. Write two sentences on which pair you would present to an investment committee and why.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why is blended CAC lower than paid CAC, and why does it matter?* → Blended divides total spend by all customers including organic ones you did not pay for. It flatters efficiency and hides whether paid acquisition works at all — which is the question an investor is asking.
2. *LTV:CAC is 4:1 with a 24-month payback. Concerned?* → Yes. The ratio is healthy and the cash position is not: you fund two years of a customer's cost before recovering it, which limits growth to your balance sheet regardless of the ratio.
3. *What is the most common LTV manipulation?* → An optimistic retention assumption derived from too short a history, sometimes with revenue rather than gross margin. Both inflate the number without changing anything real.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Accepting an LTV:CAC ratio without asking how LTV was calculated. It is the most assumption-dependent number in a pitch deck and the least often interrogated.

## Day 22 · Contribution margin and payback
**~75 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *Contribution margin is where a business model is either real or not.*

**Why today:** Revenue growth is easy to manufacture. Contribution margin is where you find out whether the transactions actually make money — and it is the number Indian consumer businesses most often lack.

**Today's topics**
1. **Contribution margin per unit** — revenue minus all variable cost. What each transaction contributes toward fixed costs.
2. **What counts as variable** — cost of goods, payment processing, delivery, support, and the discount that is really a cost.
3. **Discounts as negative revenue** — a business "profitable before discounts" is not profitable.
4. **Contribution margin 1, 2 and 3** — the layered convention used in Indian consumer and commerce businesses, and what each layer adds.
5. **The path to positive** — which costs fall with scale and which do not. Delivery cost per order often does not.
6. **Payback period calculated properly** — CAC divided by monthly contribution margin, not by revenue.

**Read & watch**
- **A published Indian D2C or quick-commerce contribution margin breakdown** · *Why this one: several Indian companies have disclosed CM1/CM2/CM3 in filings or investor material. Reading a real one beats a template.*
- **a16z on marketplace and commerce metrics** · *Why this one: the distinction between gross revenue, net revenue and contribution, which is where commerce businesses are most often misread.*
- **A filed DRHP of an Indian consumer company — the financial discussion** · *Why this one: real disclosed unit costs, in a document that had to survive a regulator. Free, and better than any secondary source.*
- **David Skok on payback period** · *Why this one: the arithmetic and the cash-flow implication, clearly.*

**Today's challenge · ~30 min**
Build a contribution margin model for a delivery business: order value, take rate, payment cost, delivery cost, discount, support cost. Calculate CM1, CM2 and CM3. Then find the order volume at which the business breaks even on fixed costs, and identify which single variable cost, if halved, most changes the answer.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why is discount a cost rather than lower revenue?* → Either treatment is defensible if applied consistently, but discount is a deliberate acquisition or retention spend and treating it as a cost makes that visible. Companies that net it into revenue are usually hiding its size.
2. *Which costs genuinely fall with scale?* → Fixed costs per unit, and some negotiated input costs. Delivery cost per order, payment processing and support per ticket often do not — which is why some businesses never reach positive contribution.
3. *Payback of CAC ÷ monthly revenue versus CAC ÷ monthly contribution margin. Which is right?* → Contribution margin. Revenue-based payback ignores the cost of serving the customer and understates the period, often by a factor of two or more.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Presenting a path to profitability that depends on fixed cost leverage while contribution margin is negative. If each transaction loses money, volume makes it worse, and no amount of scale fixes it.

## Day 23 · Cohort retention and what a growth chart hides
**~85 min · 35 pts**

**Principle:** *Cumulative charts always go up. That is why founders use them.*

**Why today:** This is the analytical skill that most distinguishes someone who can assess a startup from someone who can only read its deck. Cohort analysis is where growth is either real or an illusion created by acquisition spend.

**Today's topics**
1. **What a cohort is** — customers grouped by when they arrived, tracked forward separately.
2. **Building a retention table** — cohorts down, months since acquisition across.
3. **Reading one** — is retention improving for later cohorts, flattening, or decaying to zero?
4. **The flattening question** — a curve that flattens above zero means a durable business. One that decays to zero means you are renting customers.
5. **Revenue retention versus logo retention** — a company can lose customers and grow revenue, and the reverse.
6. **What cumulative charts conceal** — total users always rises; the cohort table shows whether anyone stays.
7. **Cohort quality over time** — later cohorts are often worse, because the cheapest customers were acquired first.

**Read & watch**
- **a16z on cohort analysis and the "growth accounting" material** · *Why this one: the clearest free explanation of why cumulative charts mislead, with real examples.*
- **A First Round Review or Reforge free essay on retention curves** · *Why this one: the flattening question treated properly — it is the single most important thing in this day.*
- **A published cohort table from a company's investor materials** · *Why this one: several listed Indian internet companies disclose cohort data. Read a real one and form a view before reading the commentary.*
- **Your Data analyst roadmap, day 47** · *Why this one: if the learner has done it, the cohort SQL is already familiar and this is the finance application of it.*

**Today's challenge · ~35 min**
Build a cohort retention table in Excel from a transaction list — cohorts down, months across. Then answer three questions in writing: does retention flatten, are later cohorts better or worse than earlier ones, and what would you want to see before believing the growth is durable.

**Check yourself**
1. *A retention curve flattens at 40% after month six. What does that tell you?* → Roughly 40% of each cohort is durable. That is a real business with a calculable long-run value, unlike a curve heading toward zero.
2. *Total users are rising and every cohort decays to zero. What is happening?* → The company is buying growth. Total rises only while acquisition spend rises, and it stops the moment spending does.
3. *Why are later cohorts often worse?* → The earliest customers are usually the most motivated and cheapest to reach. As acquisition scales, you reach less-motivated customers at higher cost — so declining cohort quality is normal and worth quantifying, not hiding.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Reading the cumulative growth chart in the deck and stopping. It is the one chart in a pitch that cannot go down, which is precisely why it is on slide four.

## Day 24 · SaaS metrics — MRR, ARR, NRR, churn
**~80 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *Net revenue retention above 100% is the single strongest signal in SaaS.*

**Why today:** SaaS is the dominant Indian startup category selling to global markets, and its metrics have specific definitions that are widely misquoted.

**Today's topics**
1. **MRR and ARR** — and why ARR quoted on one strong month is not ARR.
2. **The MRR bridge** — new, expansion, contraction, churn. The four movements that explain any change.
3. **Logo churn versus revenue churn** — losing small customers while growing large ones is a good quarter that looks like a bad one.
4. **Net revenue retention** — the metric that matters most. Above 100% means the existing base grows without new customers.
5. **Gross revenue retention** — the floor, which NRR can disguise.
6. **The magic number and CAC ratio** — efficiency of sales spend.
7. **The manipulations** — annualising a good month, counting non-recurring revenue as recurring, excluding certain churn.

**Read & watch**
- **David Skok's SaaS metrics writing** · *Why this one: the most cited free treatment of the SaaS metric set. Read the NRR and churn sections carefully.*
- **a16z — SaaS metrics material** · *Why this one: the investor's view of which metrics they weight, and which they discount.*
- **A listed SaaS company's investor presentation** — Indian or global · *Why this one: see the definitions a company that must be accurate uses. Public companies define their terms; private ones often do not.*
- **A piece on NRR benchmarks by segment** · *Why this one: NRR expectations differ substantially between SMB and enterprise, and quoting a single benchmark is a mistake.*

**Today's challenge · ~30 min**
Build an MRR bridge for twelve months from a subscription list: opening MRR, new, expansion, contraction, churn, closing. Then calculate gross and net revenue retention. Write two sentences on what the gap between them tells you.

**Check yourself**
1. *NRR is 115% and GRR is 85%. What does that say?* → Substantial churn, offset by strong expansion within the surviving base. The business grows from existing customers but leaks badly, and the leak is invisible if you only look at NRR.
2. *Why is annualising a strong month misleading?* → ARR should reflect the recurring run rate, not the best month multiplied by twelve. Founders annualise peaks; the correction usually appears in diligence.
3. *What is the most common recurring-revenue manipulation?* → Counting one-off implementation, setup or services revenue as recurring. It inflates ARR and the multiple applied to it, which compounds the error in valuation.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Accepting an ARR figure without asking for the definition. In private companies ARR is not a standardised term, and two companies quoting the same number may be measuring different things.

## Day 25 · Marketplace and D2C metrics
**~75 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *GMV is a vanity metric until you know the take rate.*

**Why today:** Marketplaces and D2C dominate Indian consumer startups, and their metrics differ from SaaS in ways that matter. A GMV number without a take rate is a headline, not information.

**Today's topics**
1. **GMV, net revenue and the take rate** — the relationship, and why GMV is quoted most.
2. **Liquidity** — the metric that actually determines whether a marketplace works. Fill rate, match rate, time to transaction.
3. **The chicken-and-egg problem** — and how it shows in the numbers before the narrative admits it.
4. **Repeat rate and order frequency** — the D2C equivalent of retention.
5. **Returns and cancellations** — deducted from GMV or not, and the effect on the true number.
6. **Category mix** — a shift toward lower-margin categories can grow GMV while shrinking contribution.
7. **AOV** — and why raising it via bundling differs from raising it via price.

**Read & watch**
- **a16z on marketplace metrics** · *Why this one: liquidity as the core marketplace metric, explained by people who have funded many. Free.*
- **An Indian e-commerce company's DRHP — the operational metrics section** · *Why this one: real GMV, take rate, repeat rate and return rate, disclosed to a regulator.*
- **A First Round Review piece on D2C unit economics** · *Why this one: the practitioner view on returns and repeat rate, which the frameworks understate.*
- **A published breakdown of an Indian quick-commerce or D2C P&L** · *Why this one: the category mix effect made concrete.*

**Today's challenge · ~30 min**
Model a marketplace: GMV, take rate, return rate, net revenue, contribution margin. Then model a scenario where GMV grows 40% but category mix shifts toward a lower-take-rate category. Show what happens to net revenue and contribution, and write one sentence on what the headline GMV growth concealed.

**Check yourself**
1. *GMV grew 50% and net revenue grew 20%. What happened?* → Take rate fell — from mix shift toward lower-commission categories, competitive pressure, or discounting funded by the platform. Growth was bought.
2. *What is marketplace liquidity and why does it matter more than GMV?* → The probability that a listing transacts, and how quickly. Low liquidity means both sides have a bad experience and churn, regardless of how large GMV is.
3. *Why does return rate matter more in India than in some markets?* → Cash on delivery and high return rates in certain categories mean reported GMV can substantially exceed delivered, retained revenue.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Comparing a marketplace's GMV to a SaaS company's ARR. They are not the same kind of number — GMV is transaction volume the company does not keep, and the comparable figure is net revenue.

## Day 26 · Burn, runway and the calculation that matters most
**~75 min · 35 pts**

**Principle:** *Runway is the only number a founder must never be wrong about.*

**Why today:** Everything else in this module informs a valuation. This one determines whether the company survives to be valued — and it is the calculation an adviser is asked for most often and most urgently.

**Today's topics**
1. **Gross burn versus net burn** — total spend versus spend minus revenue. Investors ask for net; the distinction matters when revenue is volatile.
2. **Runway** — cash divided by net burn, and why a single average is inadequate.
3. **Modelling runway properly** — month by month with actual timing, not a flat monthly average. Annual payments, tax and salary revisions cluster.
4. **The burn multiple** — net burn divided by net new ARR. How much you spend to add a rupee of recurring revenue.
5. **Default alive versus default dead** — whether current growth reaches profitability before cash runs out.
6. **When to start raising** — working backward from runway, because a round takes three to six months.
7. **What extends runway** — and the order in which levers are actually pulled.

**Read & watch**
- **Paul Graham — "Default Alive or Default Dead"** · *Why this one: the clearest statement of the question a founder must be able to answer. Short, free, and it reframes the whole calculation.*
- **YC Library — material on managing runway** · *Why this one: the practical framing of when to raise and how long it takes.*
- **A published piece on the burn multiple** · *Why this one: the efficiency metric that became standard, and how to calculate it.*
- **Damodaran on cash burn and survival probability** · *Why this one: connects runway directly to the survival probability you used in module 3. The link is not obvious and it is important.*

**Today's challenge · ~30 min**
Build a monthly cash model for eighteen months: opening cash, revenue, costs with realistic timing including annual payments, closing cash. Calculate runway. Then answer the default alive question, and identify the month a fundraise would need to start given a four-month process.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why is a single average burn figure inadequate?* → Costs are lumpy. Annual insurance, advance tax, appraisal cycles and one-off payments cluster in particular months, and an average can show four months of runway when the real answer is two.
2. *What does the burn multiple tell you that runway does not?* → Efficiency. Two companies with identical runway can differ greatly in how much they spend per rupee of new ARR, and that difference predicts the next round's difficulty.
3. *When should a founder start raising?* → Working back from runway, allowing three to six months for the process, and leaving a buffer to negotiate rather than accept. Starting with four months of cash means accepting whatever is offered.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Calculating runway on average burn and discovering the shortfall in a month with an annual payment in it. Monthly timing is not a refinement; it is the calculation.

## Day 27 · How each of these gets gamed
**~85 min · 40 pts**

**Principle:** *Every metric in this module has a standard manipulation. Learn all of them.*

**Why today:** The module closes by turning around. You have learned to calculate these; today is learning to detect when someone else has calculated them favourably — which is what advisory work actually consists of.

**Today's topics**
1. **The manipulation catalogue** — the standard adjustment for every metric covered in days 21–26.
2. **CAC** — blended instead of paid, excluding salaries, excluding organic-attributable spend.
3. **LTV** — optimistic retention, revenue instead of gross margin, no discounting.
4. **ARR** — annualising a peak month, counting services revenue, excluding known churn.
5. **Retention** — choosing a definition of active that flatters, resetting cohorts, cumulative charts.
6. **GMV** — including returns and cancellations, gross of discounts.
7. **Adjusted EBITDA** — the adjustments that are legitimate and the ones that are not.
8. **How to ask** — questions that surface a definition without accusing anyone.

**Read & watch**
- **a16z on metrics that mislead** — their material on the numbers investors distrust · *Why this one: written by investors listing the specific manipulations they see. The most directly useful reading in this module.*
- **A short-seller report on any listed company** · *Why this one: whatever you make of the conclusions, these are a masterclass in detecting favourable definitions. Read one for method.*
- **A DRHP's risk factors and accounting policies sections** · *Why this one: companies must disclose their definitions in a filing. Compare a company's public definition to how it describes itself in marketing.*
- **Your day 20 material on sanity-checking a valuation** · *Why this one: same skill, applied one layer earlier. Metrics feed valuation, so errors here compound upward.*

**Today's challenge · ~35 min**
Take a real pitch deck or investor update you can find. List every metric quoted. For each, write the definition you would need before you could use it, and the manipulation most likely to be present. Then write the five questions you would ask in a meeting — phrased as requests for clarification, not accusations.

**Check yourself**
1. *A deck says "ARR ₹12 crore, growing 200%". What do you ask first?* → How ARR is defined — whether it includes services or one-off revenue, and whether it is annualised from a single month. Then what the base was, since 200% from a small base is a different claim.
2. *A founder says retention is 90%. What is missing?* → Retention of what, measured how, over what period, for which cohort. Ninety percent monthly logo retention and ninety percent annual revenue retention are wildly different statements.
3. *What is the difference between a legitimate EBITDA adjustment and a manipulation?* → Legitimate adjustments remove genuinely non-recurring items — a one-time legal settlement, a restructuring. Manipulations remove recurring costs the business will keep incurring, such as ongoing marketing described as "growth investment".

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Assuming manipulation means dishonesty. Most of it is optimism plus a definition nobody standardised. Asking for the definition is normal professional practice, and treating it as an accusation is how junior advisers lose access.
