# Module 1 · The funding ladder

Six days. Where money comes from at each stage, and what the instruments actually do.

---

## Day 1 · Who invests, and at what stage
**~75 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *Every stage of capital is priced for a different kind of ignorance.*

**Why today:** You can value a company with a history. Startups have none, and the first thing to understand is not a technique — it is who is putting money in, what they are afraid of, and what return they need. Everything in modules 2 and 3 is downstream of that.

**Today's topics**
1. **The ladder** — bootstrapped, friends and family, angel, pre-seed, seed, Series A onward. Each has a different cheque size, a different diligence depth and a different question being asked.
2. **What each investor is underwriting** — an angel backs a founder, a seed fund backs a wedge, a Series A fund backs a repeatable motion. The same company is a different risk at each point.
3. **Fund economics and why they shape behaviour** — a fund returning capital in ten years cannot be patient in year eight. Portfolio construction explains most investor behaviour that looks irrational.
4. **The Indian landscape** — domestic funds, global funds with India teams, angel networks, accelerators, and how the stages map differently here.

**Read & watch**
- **Y Combinator — "How to Raise Money"** (YC YouTube) · *Why this one: it is a practitioner talk, not a lecture, and it states plainly what investors are deciding at each stage. Skip the Q&A at the end.*
- **YC Library — fundraising essays** · *Why this one: read two, not ten. The essays on what to do before raising are more useful than the ones on the raise itself.*
- **Damodaran — "Valuing Young, Start-up and Growth Companies"**, sections 1–2 only · *Why this one: the opening sections frame why conventional valuation breaks down. The rest of the paper is for day 15; do not read it yet.*
- **An Indian VC fund's public thesis page** — pick any active fund · *Why this one: reading what a fund says it invests in, then checking its actual portfolio, teaches more about stage than any framework.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Pick one Indian startup that has raised publicly. Reconstruct its funding history from press coverage and filings: stage, amount, lead investor, and approximate date. Then write two sentences on what changed about the company between two consecutive rounds that justified the second one.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why does an angel and a Series A fund value the same company differently?* → They are underwriting different risks. An angel is buying conviction in a founder with almost no evidence; a Series A investor is buying a repeatable motion with evidence. Different risk, different required return, different price.
2. *Why does a fund's age affect its behaviour toward your company?* → A fund near the end of its life needs realisations and cannot support a long build. The same fund is a different investor in year two and year eight.
3. *What is a lead investor actually providing beyond money?* → Price discovery, terms, diligence others rely on, and usually a board seat. Rounds frequently stall because nobody will lead, not because nobody will participate.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Treating "raising a round" as one thing. A pre-seed and a Series A are different transactions with different documents, different diligence and different buyers — and advice about one is often actively wrong for the other.

## Day 2 · Angels, syndicates and pre-seed in India
**~70 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *An angel is buying the founder. A Series A investor is buying the numbers.*

**Why today:** Day 1 mapped the ladder. Today is the bottom rung — the stage where most Indian startups actually raise, and the one where a finance graduate's instincts are least useful, because there is nothing to analyse.

**Today's topics**
1. **What an angel is deciding** — with no revenue and no product, the assessment is founder, market and wedge. Financial analysis contributes almost nothing at this stage, and pretending otherwise wastes everyone's time.
2. **Syndicates and angel networks** — how a lead angel aggregates cheques, and what that means for the cap table later.
3. **Accelerators** — what they take, what they give, and the honest calculation on whether the equity is worth it.
4. **The Indian angel tax history** — why the regulatory treatment of premium valuations mattered so much here, and what the current position is.
5. **What a pre-seed round actually looks like on paper** — often a SAFE or a small priced round with light documentation.

**Read & watch**
- **Stripe Atlas — guide to seed fundraising** · *Why this one: the clearest plain-English explanation of what actually happens in an early round, written for founders rather than lawyers.*
- **YC Library — early-stage fundraising essays** · *Why this one: pick the ones about investor psychology rather than mechanics; the mechanics come on day 3.*
- **A recent article on angel tax treatment in India** — search current, do not rely on anything older than the last budget · *Why this one: this specific rule has changed repeatedly and is the one piece of module 1 most likely to be out of date. Check the date on whatever you read.*
- **An Indian angel network's public process page** · *Why this one: seeing the actual screening process is more instructive than a description of it.*

**Today's challenge · ~20 min**
Write the one-paragraph case for a startup you know, addressed to an angel. Then rewrite the same paragraph addressed to a Series A investor. The difference between them is the entire lesson.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why is financial modelling almost irrelevant at pre-seed?* → There is nothing to model. A five-year projection for a pre-revenue company is a statement of ambition, and both sides know it. The assessment is founder and market.
2. *What does a syndicate do to a cap table?* → It can add many small holders, which complicates later rounds unless they are pooled into a single vehicle. Structure matters more than it appears at the time.
3. *Why do accelerators take equity at a low valuation?* → They are pricing for extremely high failure rates across a portfolio and for the value of the programme and network. Whether that trade is good depends entirely on what the founder lacks.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Applying valuation technique to a pre-seed company. A finance graduate's instinct is to build a model; the correct instinct is to assess the founder and the market and treat the price as a negotiation anchor.

## Day 3 · SAFEs and convertible notes
**~85 min · 35 pts**

**Principle:** *A convertible instrument defers the valuation argument. It does not remove it.*

**Why today:** This is the most common early-stage instrument and the one most misunderstood by people with a finance background — because it is neither debt nor equity while it is outstanding, and its effect only appears at conversion.

**Today's topics**
1. **What a SAFE is** — a right to future equity, not a loan. No interest, no maturity, no repayment.
2. **Convertible notes** — debt that converts. Interest, maturity date, and what happens if it matures without a round.
3. **The valuation cap** — the mechanism that rewards early risk, and the single most negotiated term.
4. **The discount** — and how cap and discount interact, since the investor takes the better of the two.
5. **Pre-money versus post-money SAFEs** — a change that shifted dilution meaningfully toward founders' cost, and which many people still get wrong.
6. **Why India often uses CCPS or CCD instead** — and what that changes.

**Read & watch**
- **Y Combinator — SAFE documents and the accompanying user guide** · *Why this one: read the actual document. It is short, and reading the instrument rather than a description of it is the point of today.*
- **Kirsty Nathoo (YC) — "Understanding SAFEs and Priced Equity Rounds"** (YC YouTube) · *Why this one: she works through the arithmetic on screen. This is the single most useful free video on this subject.*
- **Stripe Atlas — convertible instruments guide** · *Why this one: the clearest side-by-side of note versus SAFE.*
- **A comparison of the post-money and pre-money SAFE** · *Why this one: understand which one you are looking at before you calculate anything. The difference is not cosmetic.*

**Today's challenge · ~30 min**
A founder raises ₹2 crore on a SAFE with a ₹20 crore post-money cap and a 20% discount. The next round prices at ₹40 crore pre-money. Calculate the SAFE holder's ownership at conversion under both the cap and the discount, and state which applies. Then redo it with a pre-money cap and note what changed.

**Check yourself**
1. *A SAFE has a ₹20 crore cap and a 20% discount, and the round prices at ₹22 crore. Which applies?* → Compare both and take the more favourable to the investor. At ₹22 crore, the 20% discount gives an effective ₹17.6 crore, which beats the ₹20 crore cap. The discount applies.
2. *What happens to a convertible note that reaches maturity with no round?* → It becomes due. In practice it is usually extended or converted by negotiation, but the legal position is that the company owes money — which is why notes carry risk a SAFE does not.
3. *Why did post-money SAFEs change the dilution picture?* → A post-money SAFE fixes the investor's percentage regardless of how many other SAFEs are issued, so subsequent SAFEs dilute the founder rather than the earlier investor. Under pre-money SAFEs, the SAFE holders diluted each other.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Modelling a SAFE as debt because it looks like an instrument. It is not repaid and carries no interest — it is a future equity claim, and its only real effect appears on the cap table at conversion, which is exactly what module 2 is about.

## Day 4 · Priced equity rounds
**~80 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *A priced round is the first time somebody has to be specific.*

**Why today:** Convertibles defer the valuation. A priced round forces it, along with a full set of terms, real diligence and legal documentation. This is where your existing valuation training starts to matter again.

**Today's topics**
1. **What a priced round involves** — a valuation, a share price, a new class of shares, and a substantial document set.
2. **Pre-money and post-money** — and the arithmetic that everyone argues about. Post-money equals pre-money plus the raise; the investor's percentage is the raise divided by post-money.
3. **The share price mechanic** — the price per share, and why the option pool's timing changes it.
4. **Preferred versus ordinary shares** — what preference actually buys.
5. **The document set** — term sheet, share subscription agreement, shareholders' agreement, and what each governs.
6. **What diligence looks like at this stage** — and why it takes longer than founders expect.

**Read & watch**
- **Brad Feld — the term sheet series** (his blog) · *Why this one: he wrote a post per term, free, and it remains the clearest walkthrough available. The book covers the same ground and is paid; the blog is not.*
- **Kirsty Nathoo (YC) — priced round mechanics** · *Why this one: continues from day 3's video and shows the arithmetic for a priced round on screen.*
- **A publicly available Indian term sheet template or SHA summary** · *Why this one: reading actual clause language, even in a template, is worth more than any summary.*
- **Damodaran's data sets — industry multiples** · *Why this one: you will need a reference for what sectors trade at. His data is free, annual and used across the industry. Bookmark it now; you will use it in module 3.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
A company raises ₹10 crore at ₹40 crore pre-money. Calculate post-money, the investor's percentage, and the founder's dilution. Then redo it requiring a 10% post-money option pool created before the round, and quantify who paid for the pool.

**Check yourself**
1. *₹10 crore raised at ₹40 crore pre-money. What percentage does the investor own?* → Post-money is ₹50 crore, so the investor owns 20%.
2. *Why does the timing of the option pool matter?* → A pool created pre-money comes out of the existing shareholders' stake and lowers the effective price the investor pays. Created post-money, everyone including the new investor is diluted. It is a real economic term disguised as an administrative one.
3. *What does a liquidation preference actually do in a modest exit?* → It determines who gets paid first. With a 1x preference and an exit near the last valuation, preferred holders can take most or all of the proceeds before ordinary shareholders see anything.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Negotiating hard on valuation and accepting the option pool and preference terms as standard. The pool shift and the preference structure frequently move more economics than the headline number does.

## Day 5 · Indian instruments — CCPS and CCD
**~70 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *The Indian structure differs from the Silicon Valley template, and the template is what everyone reads.*

**Why today:** Almost every free resource on startup fundraising is American. If you are advising Indian companies, the instruments, the regulator and the constraints are different — and this is the day that gap gets closed.

**Today's topics**
1. **Why CCPS is the standard Indian instrument** — compulsorily convertible preference shares, and the regulatory reasons they dominate here.
2. **CCDs** — compulsorily convertible debentures and when they are used instead.
3. **FEMA and foreign investment** — pricing guidelines, sectoral caps, and the reporting obligations that follow a foreign round.
4. **Why plain SAFEs are less common in India** — and what gets used in their place.
5. **Valuation certificates** — the requirement that a merchant banker or registered valuer supports the price in certain transactions.

**Read & watch**
- **RBI — FEMA provisions on foreign investment in Indian companies** · *Why this one: the primary source. Dense, but the pricing guidelines section is the part that binds a real transaction.*
- **An Indian law firm's public explainer on CCPS versus equity** · *Why this one: firms publish these free for marketing, and they are usually accurate and current. Check the publication date.*
- **MCA filings for a funded Indian startup** · *Why this one: look at what was actually filed. The instrument, the price and the terms are in the public record, and reading one real filing beats reading three explainers.*
- **A recent summary of foreign investment reporting requirements** · *Why this one: the compliance calendar is part of the advice, and it changes.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Find one funded Indian startup's MCA filings. Identify the instrument used, the number of shares, and the price. Then write three sentences on why that instrument was chosen over ordinary equity.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why "compulsorily" convertible?* → Optionally convertible instruments are treated as debt under FEMA and attract external commercial borrowing rules. Compulsory conversion makes it equity for regulatory purposes.
2. *What does a valuation certificate do?* → It supports the price at which shares are issued, which regulators require in certain transactions. The valuation must be defensible to someone other than the parties.
3. *Why does a foreign investor's presence change the process?* → FEMA pricing guidelines, sectoral caps and reporting obligations apply. A round that would be simple domestically acquires a compliance layer.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Reading American fundraising material and assuming it applies. The economics translate; the instruments, the regulator and the timeline do not.

## Day 6 · Debt, venture debt and revenue-based finance
**~75 min · 35 pts**

**Principle:** *Debt is cheaper until the month it is not available.*

**Why today:** Equity is not the only option, and knowing when debt is appropriate is part of advising well. It also closes the ladder: you now know every instrument a startup might use.

**Today's topics**
1. **Venture debt** — what it is, who provides it in India, and what it costs including warrants.
2. **When debt makes sense** — extending runway between rounds, financing working capital, avoiding dilution at a low valuation.
3. **When it does not** — pre-revenue, unpredictable cash flows, or as a substitute for a round that will not happen.
4. **Revenue-based financing** — repayment as a share of revenue, and what it costs in effective terms.
5. **Working capital facilities and invoice discounting** — the unglamorous instruments that solve real problems.
6. **The covenant risk** — what happens when a covenant breaks.

**Read & watch**
- **An Indian venture debt provider's public explainer** · *Why this one: the providers explain their own product clearly, and reading two of them shows you where the terms actually vary.*
- **First Round Review or a16z on venture debt** · *Why this one: the investor perspective on when debt helps and when it accelerates a failure.*
- **A revenue-based financing provider's terms page** · *Why this one: calculate the effective annualised cost yourself. It is frequently higher than it looks, and that calculation is the exercise.*
- **Damodaran — cost of capital material** · *Why this one: you know this from your degree. Revisit it specifically in the context of a company with no stable cash flows, where the standard framework strains.*

**Today's challenge · ~30 min**
A startup with ₹4 crore ARR is offered ₹3 crore of venture debt at 14% over 24 months with 1% warrants, or an equity round of ₹3 crore at ₹30 crore pre-money. Model both. Calculate the founder's ownership under each and state the conditions under which each is the better choice.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why do venture debt providers take warrants?* → The interest rate alone does not compensate for the risk of lending to an unprofitable company. Warrants give upside exposure if the company succeeds.
2. *Why is venture debt usually raised alongside or just after equity, rather than instead of it?* → Lenders underwrite the equity investors' willingness to fund again. A company with no recent round and no clear next one is a much harder credit.
3. *What is the real risk of a covenant in a startup facility?* → Breaching one can trigger acceleration at exactly the moment the company cannot repay. The covenant matters more than the rate.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Comparing debt and equity on cost alone. Debt is cheaper on every spreadsheet and carries a repayment obligation that equity does not — and the relevant question is what happens in the bad case, not the base case.
