# Startup Finance & Fundraising — 48 days

**~10 weeks · 7 modules · 48 days · ~52 hours · intermediate · Business & growth**

For finance graduates moving into startup and SME advisory. Assumes you can already read a balance sheet and build a DCF. Covers what a PGDM does not: cap tables, term sheets, valuing a company with no cash flows, diligence from the investor's side, and the Indian SME IPO route.

> **Before publishing:** every source below is real and, to my knowledge, free — but must be opened and verified, and the specific sections confirmed. SEBI and exchange documents change with each circular. Set `reviewed_at` on completion and review quarterly.

**Prerequisites:** `roadmap_prereqs` → *suggests* Excel at work · *assumes* financial statements, DCF and comparables

**48 days over 10 weeks is 4.8 days a week.** Slightly under the 5–7 target, so either compress to 9 weeks or add 6 days to the diligence and IPO modules. Do not stretch it to 12 weeks — the streak breaks.

---

## Why this roadmap exists

Our first clearly-defined potential customer is a PGDM Finance graduate going into startup and SME IPO advisory. Their declared knowledge covers fourteen concepts we already model — time value of money through to M&A. Their declared gap is fifteen more, and eleven they did not name but will need.

**The pattern is worth stating:** a finance degree teaches you to value a company with a history. Startup advisory requires valuing one without, and auditing a founder's model rather than building your own. Nothing in a PGDM covers that, and there is no sequenced free curriculum for it anywhere.

**Category impact:** Business & growth currently holds one roadmap. This makes two, and it is the first thing in the catalogue aimed at a commerce graduate rather than an engineer.

## Module 1 · The funding ladder — days 1–6

Where money comes from at each stage, and what the instruments actually do.

| Day | Title | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who invests, and at what stage | *Every stage of capital is priced for a different kind of ignorance.* |
| 2 | Angels, syndicates and pre-seed in India | *An angel is buying the founder. A Series A investor is buying the numbers.* |
| 3 | SAFEs and convertible notes | *A convertible instrument defers the valuation argument. It does not remove it.* |
| 4 | Priced equity rounds | *A priced round is the first time somebody has to be specific.* |
| 5 | Indian instruments — CCPS and CCD | *The Indian structure differs from the Silicon Valley template, and the template is what everyone reads.* |
| 6 | Debt, venture debt and revenue-based finance | *Debt is cheaper until the month it is not available.* |

**Deliverable:** a one-page comparison of five instruments — dilution, control, downside, and when each is the right answer.

## Module 2 · Cap tables and dilution — days 7–13

The mechanical skill most finance graduates lack entirely.

| Day | Title | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | What a cap table actually is | *If you cannot rebuild the cap table from the documents, you do not understand the deal.* |
| 8 | Pre-money, post-money and the arithmetic | *Pre-money and post-money differ by exactly the amount everyone argues about.* |
| 9 | Building a cap table from scratch in Excel | *A cap table with a hardcoded ownership percentage is a cap table that will be wrong next round.* |
| 10 | Modelling a round and its dilution | *Founders track their percentage. They should track their value.* |
| 11 | ESOP pools — sizing, timing and who pays | *Who the pool dilutes depends entirely on whether it sits pre-money or post-money.* |
| 12 | Convertible conversion at the next round | *A SAFE's discount and cap interact, and the interaction surprises people at conversion.* |
| 13 | Waterfall and exit distribution | *Liquidation preference decides who gets paid first, and in a modest exit that is everyone's answer.* |

**Deliverable:** a working cap table model that takes a SAFE, converts it at a priced round, creates a 10% ESOP pool, and produces an exit waterfall — all from formulas, no hardcoded percentages.

## Module 3 · Valuing a company with no cash flows — days 14–20

Where their existing DCF knowledge gets extended rather than replaced.

| Day | Title | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| 14 | Why textbook DCF fails on a startup | *A DCF on a pre-revenue company is a spreadsheet-shaped opinion.* |
| 15 | Damodaran on young companies | *You cannot avoid the uncertainty. You can only make it explicit.* |
| 16 | Revenue and EBITDA multiples | *A multiple is a DCF with the assumptions hidden inside it.* |
| 17 | Choosing a defensible comparable set | *The peer set is where the valuation is really decided.* |
| 18 | Scorecard, Berkus and the VC method | *These are negotiation anchors, not valuations. Know which you are doing.* |
| 19 | Valuation in a negotiation | *The number is an output of leverage as much as of analysis.* |
| 20 | Sanity-checking someone else's valuation | *Find the one assumption carrying the whole answer. There always is one.* |

**Deliverable:** value one real Indian startup three ways — a scenario DCF, a revenue multiple, and the VC method — and write one page on why the numbers differ and which you would defend.

## Module 4 · Unit economics and traction — days 21–27

The layer between the pitch narrative and the financial model.

| Day | Title | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| 21 | CAC, LTV and the ratio everyone quotes wrongly | *An LTV:CAC of 3 means nothing without the payback period beside it.* |
| 22 | Contribution margin and payback | *Contribution margin is where a business model is either real or not.* |
| 23 | Cohort retention and what a growth chart hides | *Cumulative charts always go up. That is why founders use them.* |
| 24 | SaaS metrics — MRR, ARR, NRR, churn | *Net revenue retention above 100% is the single strongest signal in SaaS.* |
| 25 | Marketplace and D2C metrics | *GMV is a vanity metric until you know the take rate.* |
| 26 | Burn, runway and the calculation that matters most | *Runway is the only number a founder must never be wrong about.* |
| 27 | How each of these gets gamed | *Every metric in this module has a standard manipulation. Learn all of them.* |

**Deliverable:** take a real startup's public numbers, build the unit economics, and identify the two metrics you would want before investing.

| Day | Title | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| 28 | What a deck must prove, slide by slide | *Ten slides, and each one answers an objection rather than making a claim.* |
| 29 | Market sizing bottom-up | *A TAM copied from a consulting report tells an investor you did not do the work.* |
| 30 | The narrative and the numbers agreeing | *If the deck and the model disagree, the investor believes neither.* |
| 31 | Building a driver-based startup model | *A model with a blended growth rate is a model nobody can interrogate.* |
| 32 | Scenario and sensitivity in a fundraising model | *Show the downside yourself. The investor will find it anyway.* |
| 33 | The data room | *Diligence speed is a signal. A disorganised data room reads as a disorganised company.* |

**Deliverable:** a complete driver-based operating model plus a ten-slide deck whose numbers reconcile to it exactly.

## Module 7 · SME IPO and the Indian regulatory layer — days 42–48

The India-specific module, and the one that rots fastest.

| Day | Title | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| 42 | Why an SME lists at all | *An IPO is a financing decision, not a milestone.* |
| 43 | NSE Emerge and BSE SME — eligibility | *The eligibility criteria decide the timeline more than anything the company does.* |
| 44 | The DRHP, section by section | *The risk factors are the only part written by lawyers protecting themselves. Read them first.* |
| 45 | Reading a real DRHP critically | *A filed DRHP is the most detailed free document about any company you will ever get.* |
| 46 | The process — merchant banker to listing | *Every party in an IPO is paid at a different milestone. That explains most of the behaviour.* |
| 47 | Migration to the main board | *The SME platform is a staircase, and the second step has its own criteria.* |
| 48 | SEBI ICDR and the adviser's obligations | *Knowing where the rule binds you is part of the advice.* |

**Deliverable:** read one filed DRHP end to end and produce a two-page critical summary — the business, the risks that matter, and the three questions you would ask management.
