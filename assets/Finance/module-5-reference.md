## Day 28 · What a deck must prove, slide by slide
**~75 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *Ten slides, and each one answers an objection rather than making a claim.*

**Why today:** A pitch deck is not a document about a company. It is a sequence of answers to the questions an investor asks in order, and understanding that order is the whole craft.

**Today's topics**
1. **The standard sequence** — problem, solution, market, product, traction, business model, competition, team, financials, ask. Deviations should be deliberate.
2. **The objection behind each slide** — "is this a real problem", "why you", "is it big enough", "does anyone want it", "can you keep them".
3. **Why traction moves early in a deck that has it** — and what to lead with when it does not.
4. **The team slide at seed versus Series A** — at seed it is the investment case; later it is a checkbox.
5. **The ask** — amount, use of funds, and the milestone it buys. An ask without a milestone is a number without a reason.
6. **What does not belong** — NDAs, exit strategy slides, five-year revenue projections at seed stage.

**Read & watch**
- **YC Library — the deck and pitch material** · *Why this one: YC's guidance is the closest thing to a standard, and it is written from having seen an enormous volume of decks. Free.*
- **Sequoia's published pitch deck template** · *Why this one: the original ten-slide structure most decks still follow. Short and free.*
- **A collection of published successful decks** — Airbnb, Buffer and others are widely available · *Why this one: read three real decks in full before writing anything. Note what is absent from them.*
- **Y Combinator — "How to Design a Better Pitch Deck"** (YC YouTube) · *Why this one: specific, practitioner-delivered advice on the sequence, not general design tips.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Take a published deck from a company that raised successfully. For each slide, write the one objection it exists to answer. Then identify the two slides doing the most work, and the one you would cut.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why does traction move earlier when a company has it?* → Because it answers several objections at once — real problem, real demand, some execution ability. A company with traction leads with it; one without must earn attention through problem and team.
2. *What is wrong with a five-year revenue projection at seed?* → It cannot be credible and both sides know it. It signals either naivety or that you think the reader is naive. Show the model on request; do not lead with it.
3. *What makes an ask credible?* → Amount, use of funds, and the specific milestone it reaches — "eighteen months to ₹5 crore ARR and a Series A". An amount alone invites the question you should have answered.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Building the deck as a description of the company rather than a sequence of answers. The result reads as complete and persuades nobody, because it never engages the question the reader is actually holding.

## Day 29 · Market sizing bottom-up
**~85 min · 35 pts**

**Principle:** *A TAM copied from a consulting report tells an investor you did not do the work.*

**Why today:** Market size is the slide most often outsourced to a headline number, and the one where doing it properly is most visible. It is also a genuinely transferable analytical skill.

**Today's topics**
1. **TAM, SAM, SOM** — total addressable, serviceable addressable, serviceable obtainable. Nested, and each requires a different argument.
2. **Bottom-up construction** — units × price × frequency. Start from a countable thing.
3. **Top-down and why it fails** — "the Indian retail market is $X and we need 1%" is not an argument, it is a wish.
4. **Finding the countable unit** — number of businesses, households, transactions, professionals. This choice determines everything downstream.
5. **Indian data sources** — census, MCA, government statistics, industry bodies, exchange filings.
6. **Sanity-checking against revenue** — if your SOM implies a revenue that no company in the sector has achieved, revisit it.
7. **Presenting it** — show the build, not the conclusion.

**Read & watch**
- **A YC or First Round piece on market sizing** · *Why this one: the investor's view on why bottom-up is expected and top-down is discounted.*
- **Damodaran on total addressable market and its abuse in valuation** · *Why this one: he has written specifically on how TAM inflation drives valuation errors. Connects directly to module 3.*
- **An Indian government statistical source** — MoSPI, census data, or an RBI publication · *Why this one: learn where real Indian denominators come from. Most decks use none.*
- **A filed DRHP's industry section** · *Why this one: companies must support market claims in a filing. See what a defensible market argument looks like when a regulator will read it.*

**Today's challenge · ~35 min**
Size the Indian market for one specific B2B product, bottom-up. Start from a countable unit and build to TAM, SAM and SOM, showing every step and citing each number's source. Then write two sentences on which assumption in your build is weakest.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why do investors discount top-down market sizing?* → Because it proves nothing. Every large market contains a 1% that nobody has reached, and the number tells you nothing about whether this company can reach it.
2. *What is the difference between SAM and SOM?* → SAM is the portion of the total market your product could serve given its capabilities and geography. SOM is what you could realistically win in a defined period given competition and your capacity.
3. *Your SOM implies ₹500 crore revenue in year five. How do you check it?* → Against the largest revenue any company in that sector has achieved, and in what timeframe. If your SOM exceeds the sector leader's actual revenue, the build has an error in it.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Presenting the conclusion without the build. A large number with no visible construction is treated as decoration, whereas a smaller number with a defensible build is treated as analysis.

## Day 30 · The narrative and the numbers agreeing
**~70 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *If the deck and the model disagree, the investor believes neither.*

**Why today:** This is the failure mode that kills otherwise good raises. The deck says one thing, the model says another, and the inconsistency is discovered in the second meeting.

**Today's topics**
1. **Where deck and model diverge** — the deck is updated after the model, or by a different person, or after feedback that was never carried back.
2. **The numbers that must reconcile exactly** — current revenue, growth rate, headcount, burn, runway, the ask.
3. **Rounding and presentation** — consistent units, consistent periods, consistent definitions across both.
4. **The one-source rule** — every number in the deck traces to a cell in the model. Nothing is typed twice.
5. **Version control** — decks circulate; models get updated. A deck sent three weeks ago is still being read.
6. **What an investor does when they find a discrepancy** — they stop evaluating the business and start evaluating your care.

**Read & watch**
- **A YC Library piece on fundraising process and materials** · *Why this one: the practical mechanics of what gets sent and when, which is where version problems originate.*
- **The FAST modelling standard or equivalent free modelling guidance** · *Why this one: the discipline of a single source for every number is a modelling convention, not a fundraising one. Learn it as a standard.*
- **ExcelJet — linking and named ranges** · *Why this one: the mechanical means of implementing the one-source rule.*
- **A First Round Review piece on fundraising mistakes** · *Why this one: practitioner accounts of losing a round on process rather than substance.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Take any deck you can find with financial claims. Build a one-page reconciliation listing every number in it, where it should come from, and whether the deck's numbers are internally consistent. Note every place two slides imply different things.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why is a discrepancy worse than a weak number?* → A weak number is a business problem you can discuss. A discrepancy is a competence signal, and it makes every other number suspect.
2. *What does the one-source rule mean in practice?* → Every figure in the deck is linked to or copied from a single identified cell in the model. When the model updates, you know exactly which slides change.
3. *Why does version control matter in a raise?* → Decks circulate for weeks. If you update the model and the deck separately, someone is reading a version that no longer matches what you will say in the meeting.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Updating the model after investor feedback and not carrying the change back through the deck. It is the single most common source of second-meeting discrepancies.

## Day 31 · Building a driver-based startup model
**~95 min · 40 pts**

**Principle:** *A model with a blended growth rate is a model nobody can interrogate.*

**Why today:** The build day, and the point where module 4's drivers become a model. This is also where their existing three-statement modelling connects to startup work — the structure is familiar, the drivers are not.

**Today's topics**
1. **Drivers versus growth rates** — revenue as customers × price × frequency, never as last year plus a percentage.
2. **The driver tree** — marketing spend → CAC → new customers → retention → active customers → revenue. Each link is an assumption someone can argue with.
3. **Separating inputs, calculations and outputs** — one input block, no assumptions buried in formulas.
4. **Building forward from module 4** — CAC, contribution margin and retention become the model's engine.
5. **Headcount and cost modelling** — driven by the plan, not by a percentage of revenue.
6. **Linking to cash** — the P&L is not the point; the cash line is.
7. **Modelling monthly for eighteen months, annually thereafter** — precision where it matters, direction where it does not.

**Read & watch**
- **A published startup financial model template** — several VCs and accelerators publish free ones · *Why this one: open two and compare their driver structures before building your own. Do not use one; borrow the structure.*
- **David Skok or a16z on driver-based SaaS modelling** · *Why this one: the connection between unit economics and a forecast, which is exactly the module 4 to module 5 bridge.*
- **Damodaran's spreadsheets** · *Why this one: his models are unusually well structured and free. Look at how he separates inputs from calculations.*
- **Microsoft Learn — Excel scenario tools** · *Why this one: needed for tomorrow, and worth setting up today.*

**Today's challenge · ~45 min**
Build an eighteen-month driver-based model using your module 4 numbers: marketing spend, CAC, new customers, retention, active customers, revenue, contribution margin, fixed costs, net burn, closing cash. Every output must be a formula. Then change CAC by 20% and confirm every downstream figure updates, including runway.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why model revenue as customers × price rather than as a growth rate?* → Because each component is separately arguable. A 15% growth rate cannot be challenged specifically; a CAC assumption or a retention rate can, and that is what makes a model useful in a conversation.
2. *Why monthly for eighteen months and annual after?* → Cash timing matters in the near term and precision beyond eighteen months is false. Modelling year five monthly implies a confidence nobody has.
3. *What breaks when marketing spend rises 50% in a driver-based model?* → New customers rise, but so does burn, and runway shortens. If CAC also rises with scale — which it usually does — the model should reflect that rather than holding CAC constant.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Building the model in the P&L format their degree taught, then bolting drivers on afterwards. The driver tree is the model; the P&L is a presentation of it.

## Day 32 · Scenario and sensitivity in a fundraising model
**~80 min · 35 pts**

**Principle:** *Show the downside yourself. The investor will find it anyway.*

**Why today:** A single-scenario model implies a confidence that undermines it. Scenarios convert a forecast from a prediction into an argument about what matters.

**Today's topics**
1. **Three scenarios, not five** — base, downside, upside. More than three is noise.
2. **What changes between them** — the two or three drivers that actually matter, not every input.
3. **One-way and two-way sensitivity tables** — where the output is most exposed.
4. **Identifying the load-bearing driver** — change each input by 10% and rank the effect. Usually one or two dominate.
5. **The downside case that matters** — not "growth is slower" but "what happens to runway if CAC rises 40% and retention falls 10 points".
6. **Presenting it** — lead with the driver ranking, not with three columns of numbers.
7. **Why founders resist this** — and why showing the downside builds more credibility than hiding it.

**Read & watch**
- **Damodaran on scenario analysis and simulation in valuation** · *Why this one: the rigorous treatment, and it connects to the scenario valuation from module 3 day 15.*
- **ExcelJet — data tables, Goal Seek, Scenario Manager** · *Why this one: the mechanics. One careful read is enough.*
- **A YC or First Round piece on presenting a model to investors** · *Why this one: the practitioner view on what investors do with a model once they have it.*
- **Your module 3 day 14 material on terminal value sensitivity** · *Why this one: same technique, applied to a different question. The skill transfers.*

**Today's challenge · ~35 min**
Extend yesterday's model with three scenarios and a two-way sensitivity table on runway across CAC and retention. Rank all your drivers by their effect on eighteen-month closing cash. Then write one paragraph identifying the single assumption the whole model rests on, and what you would want to see to believe it.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why three scenarios rather than five?* → Because a reader compares three and skims five. The purpose is to show the range and the drivers, not to enumerate possibilities.
2. *What makes a downside case useful?* → Specificity. "Revenue 30% lower" is not a scenario; "CAC rises 40% as we move beyond the initial channel, and retention falls 10 points" is a mechanism someone can assess.
3. *Why does showing the downside build credibility?* → It demonstrates you have thought about failure and know where the model is fragile. An investor will find the fragility regardless; finding it first is the difference between preparation and being caught out.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Building scenarios by changing every input at once. The result shows a range and hides the mechanism, when the mechanism is the entire value of the exercise.

## Day 33 · The data room
**~70 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *Diligence speed is a signal. A disorganised data room reads as a disorganised company.*

**Why today:** The unglamorous day that determines how long a round takes. It is also directly billable advisory work, and the thing a founder is least likely to have done.

**Today's topics**
1. **What a data room contains** — corporate, financial, commercial, legal, HR, IP, and the current cap table.
2. **The Indian document set** — incorporation documents, MCA filings, statutory registers, GST and tax filings, ESOP documentation, key contracts.
3. **Structure and naming** — folders that match the diligence checklist an investor will use, with dates in filenames.
4. **Staging access** — what goes in before a term sheet, what after.
5. **The gaps that delay rounds** — undocumented option grants, missing board resolutions, unsigned agreements, a cap table that disagrees with the register.
6. **The Q&A log** — tracking what was asked, answered and by whom.
7. **Preparing before you need it** — a founder who assembles the room during diligence adds weeks.

**Read & watch**
- **A published diligence checklist** — several Indian VCs and law firms publish theirs free · *Why this one: build the room from the checklist, not from intuition. Two checklists compared shows you the common core.*
- **Stripe Atlas — record-keeping guidance** · *Why this one: the practical discipline, explained without legal jargon.*
- **An Indian law firm's explainer on startup diligence** · *Why this one: the Indian document set differs — MCA filings, statutory registers, GST. Check the publication date.*
- **YC Library on the fundraising process timeline** · *Why this one: where diligence sits in the process, and how preparation compresses it.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Build a complete data room folder structure for an Indian startup at Series A, populated with the document names that should exist in each. Then mark which five are most commonly missing, and what each one's absence delays.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why does a disorganised data room affect the deal and not just the timeline?* → It signals how the company operates. An investor extrapolates from what they can see to what they cannot, and record-keeping is the most visible proxy for internal discipline.
2. *What are the most common gaps in an Indian startup's data room?* → Undocumented ESOP grants, missing board resolutions for past issuances, statutory registers not maintained, and a cap table that does not reconcile to MCA filings.
3. *What should go in before a term sheet?* → Enough to support the investment decision — corporate structure, financials, key metrics, cap table — while holding customer contracts and sensitive commercial detail until terms are agreed.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Assembling the data room after receiving a term sheet. Diligence then runs at the speed of document retrieval, and weeks of exclusivity are spent finding papers instead of answering questions.

## Day 34 · Auditing a founder's model
**~90 min · 45 pts**

**Principle:** *You will spend more time finding errors in models than building them.*

**Why today:** The module — and arguably the roadmap — culminates here. For anyone advising startups, this is the daily work, and no finance degree teaches it.

**Today's topics**
1. **The order to check in** — structure, then arithmetic, then logic, then assumptions. Most errors are mechanical and found in the first two passes.
2. **Structural checks** — does it balance, does cash reconcile, are there circular references, is there a check row at all.
3. **Mechanical errors** — hardcoded values inside formulas, broken SUM ranges, sign errors, inconsistent row logic across columns.
4. **Formula auditing in Excel** — trace precedents and dependents, error checking, evaluate formula.
5. **Logic errors** — a growth rate applied to the wrong base, retention compounding incorrectly, a cost that should scale and does not.
6. **Assumption interrogation** — rank drivers by effect, then question the top two rather than all twelve.
7. **How to report findings** — separate errors from disagreements, and lead with the ones that change the answer.

**Read & watch**
- **The FAST modelling standard** or equivalent free modelling guidance · *Why this one: knowing what a well-built model looks like tells you exactly where to look in a badly built one.*
- **Microsoft Learn — formula auditing tools** · *Why this one: trace precedents and evaluate formula are the two features that make this work practical rather than tedious.*
- **A published account of a modelling error with consequences** · *Why this one: spreadsheet errors have caused real institutional embarrassment, and reading one case makes the discipline stick.*
- **Your module 3 day 20 material** · *Why this one: same skill applied to a valuation rather than an operating model. Read your own notes from that day before starting.*

**Today's challenge · ~40 min**
Take a startup model — one you can find, or deliberately break your own from day 31 in six ways. Produce a one-page audit: errors found with their cell references, the effect of each on the output, the two assumptions carrying the model, and the three questions you would ask the founder. Separate errors from disagreements explicitly.

**Check yourself**
1. *In what order do you check a model?* → Structure, arithmetic, logic, assumptions. Most errors are mechanical, they are fastest to find, and there is no point interrogating an assumption in a model that does not add up.
2. *You find one hardcoded number inside a formula. Why does it matter beyond that cell?* → It indicates the model has not been audited, so there are likely others. One hardcode converts a spot check into a full review.
3. *How do you distinguish an error from a disagreement?* → An error is objectively wrong — a broken range, a sign error, a figure that does not reconcile. A disagreement is an assumption you would set differently. Reporting them together makes both easier to dismiss.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Rebuilding the model instead of auditing it. Your value is finding what is wrong and what it rests on within an hour — not producing a competing version by the end of the week.
