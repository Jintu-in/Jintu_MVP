# Startup Finance — Module 6, fully authored (owner reference, received 2026-09-05)

**Days 35–41 · Due diligence from the investor's side**

Day 34 taught auditing a spreadsheet. This module applies the same discipline to a company — and ends in the document that is the actual output of investment work.

---

## The shift this module asks for

Everything before this point has been analytical. Diligence is investigative, and the difference matters.

An analyst asks *what does this number say*. A diligence professional asks *what would have to be true for this number to be wrong, and how would I find out*. That is a different posture, and it is uncomfortable for people trained to work with audited statements where the verification has already been done by someone else.

**For the customer this roadmap serves**, this is the module that maps most directly onto billable work. Transaction advisory, VC analysis and SME IPO preparation are all this skill under different names.

## Day 35 · What diligence is actually looking for
**~75 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *Diligence is not verification. It is looking for the reason not to invest.*

**Why today:** Most people entering this work assume diligence confirms what the deck said. It does the opposite — it hunts for the thing that would change the decision, and understanding that inverts how you read everything.

**Today's topics**
1. **The purpose** — find the deal-breaker before the money moves, and price what you find that is not fatal.
2. **The workstreams** — financial, commercial, legal, technical, HR. Who runs each and how they intersect.
3. **Confirmatory versus exploratory** — later-stage diligence confirms a thesis; early-stage diligence forms one.
4. **What is proportionate** — a ₹2 crore seed round does not get the diligence of a ₹200 crore Series C, and knowing the appropriate depth is professional judgement.
5. **The three outcomes** — proceed, proceed at a different price or structure, or walk. The second is the most common and the most valuable.
6. **Where a junior person adds value** — thoroughness on the workstream they own, and flagging what they cannot resolve rather than glossing it.

**Read & watch**
- **A published VC diligence checklist** — several Indian and global funds publish theirs · *Why this one: read two and note the overlap. The common core is what diligence actually is; the differences are firm preference.*
- **A Big Four transaction services overview** — the free thought-leadership material · *Why this one: the professional framing of workstreams and scope, from firms that do it at volume.*
- **First Round Review or a16z on what investors check** · *Why this one: the investor's own account of what changes their mind, which checklists do not capture.*
- **Your module 5 day 34 notes** · *Why this one: same discipline, larger object. Read what you wrote before starting.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Take a startup you know something about. Write the five questions whose answers would most change an investment decision. For each, state how you would find the answer and what evidence would satisfy you. Rank them by how likely they are to be a problem.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why is "confirming the deck" the wrong frame?* → Because it leads to checking what you were told rather than looking for what you were not told. The material risks are usually in what was omitted.
2. *What is proportionate diligence at seed?* → Founder references, cap table, basic financials, a small number of customer conversations, and legal cleanliness. Full quality-of-earnings work on a company with eighteen months of revenue is disproportionate and slows a round for no information gain.
3. *What is the most valuable diligence outcome?* → Proceeding at a different price or structure. Finding something that changes the terms rather than killing the deal is where diligence pays for itself.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Treating diligence as a checklist to complete rather than a question to answer. The checklist is a floor. The judgement is deciding which two items deserve three days.

## Day 36 · Quality of revenue
**~80 min · 35 pts**

**Principle:** *Revenue recognised is not revenue collected, and neither is revenue retained.*

**Why today:** Revenue is the number everything else is built on, and it is the most frequently overstated figure in a startup's financials — usually through timing and definition rather than fabrication.

**Today's topics**
1. **Recognition timing** — when revenue is booked versus when cash arrives versus when the service is delivered. All three can differ legitimately.
2. **Concentration risk** — one customer at 40% of revenue is a different business from ten at 4%.
3. **Contracted versus one-off** — the proportion that recurs, and whether "recurring" means a contract or a habit.
4. **Related-party revenue** — sales to entities connected to founders or investors. Common in Indian group structures and material.
5. **Gross versus net** — whether the company is a principal or an agent determines which it may report, and the difference can be enormous.
6. **Collections and receivables ageing** — revenue with a 180-day receivable is not revenue yet.
7. **Cohort revenue** — module 4's retention work applied here as evidence.

**Read & watch**
- **A Big Four guide to quality of revenue** — free published material · *Why this one: the professional framework, and specifically the tests they apply.*
- **A filed DRHP's revenue recognition policy and related-party disclosures** · *Why this one: real disclosures a company had to make to a regulator. The related-party section is often the most interesting page in a DRHP.*
- **Ind AS 115 summary** — revenue from contracts with customers · *Why this one: the standard governing recognition in India. You do not need it in full; the five-step model is what matters.*
- **An article on gross-versus-net reporting in Indian e-commerce** · *Why this one: this specific issue has driven large restatements in Indian internet companies.*

**Today's challenge · ~35 min**
Take a company's revenue disclosure from a DRHP or annual report. Analyse: recognition policy, customer concentration, related-party proportion, receivables ageing. Write a one-page assessment of revenue quality and the three questions you would ask management.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why does gross versus net matter so much?* → A marketplace reporting gross transaction value as revenue can show ten times the figure of one reporting only its commission. Same economics, wildly different revenue line and therefore different multiple.
2. *What does customer concentration change?* → The risk profile and the valuation. Losing a customer worth 40% of revenue is an existential event, and an acquirer or investor will price that.
3. *Why is related-party revenue material?* → It may not be arm's length and may not persist after the transaction. It is revenue that could vanish precisely when the new investor arrives.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Accepting the revenue line and analysing everything below it. Almost every material financial issue in a startup originates in how revenue was defined and when it was recognised.

## Day 37 · Quality of earnings and normalisation
**~85 min · 35 pts**

**Principle:** *Every founder's EBITDA contains at least one adjustment they hope you miss.*

**Why today:** This is the core of financial diligence and the most directly transferable skill in the module — it is what Big Four transaction services teams do all day.

**Today's topics**
1. **What quality of earnings means** — the sustainable, recurring earnings a buyer is actually acquiring.
2. **Normalisation adjustments** — removing genuinely non-recurring items to arrive at a run-rate.
3. **Legitimate adjustments** — one-off legal settlements, restructuring costs, a genuine one-time event.
4. **Aggressive adjustments** — recurring marketing described as investment, founder salary below market, understated future costs.
5. **The founder salary question** — a founder paying themselves nothing inflates EBITDA by the cost of their replacement.
6. **Below-market related-party costs** — rent from a founder-owned entity, services from a connected company.
7. **Building the bridge** — reported EBITDA to adjusted EBITDA, every adjustment separately listed and defended.
8. **Why it changes the price** — the multiple applies to the adjusted number, so a ₹1 crore adjustment at 8x moves ₹8 crore of value.

**Read & watch**
- **A Big Four quality-of-earnings publication** · *Why this one: the professional method with worked adjustment categories. These firms publish detailed material free.*
- **A DRHP's restated financials section** · *Why this one: Indian IPO rules require restatement, so a DRHP shows exactly this exercise performed and disclosed.*
- **An article on adjusted EBITDA abuse** · *Why this one: the pattern of adjustments that regulators and auditors have pushed back on.*
- **Damodaran on earnings quality and normalisation** · *Why this one: connects the adjustment work back to valuation, which is where its consequence lands.*

**Today's challenge · ~35 min**
Build an EBITDA bridge for a company from reported to adjusted. Include at least six adjustments — some legitimate, some contestable. For each, state the amount, the justification, and whether you would accept it as a buyer. Then calculate the valuation impact at an 8x multiple.

**Check yourself**
1. *A founder takes no salary. Is that an adjustment?* → Yes, downward. A buyer must pay someone to do that job, so market-rate compensation is a real recurring cost that the reported EBITDA omits.
2. *"Growth marketing" excluded as a one-off investment. Accept?* → Almost never. If the business needs marketing to sustain revenue, it is recurring by definition. Adjusting it out is the most common aggressive adjustment there is.
3. *Why does a ₹1 crore adjustment matter more than ₹1 crore?* → Because the multiple applies to it. At 8x, a ₹1 crore adjustment moves ₹8 crore of enterprise value, which is why adjustments are negotiated harder than almost anything else.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Accepting adjusted EBITDA as presented. The adjustments are the negotiation, and each one should be listed, justified and separately contested.

## Day 38 · Working capital and the cash conversion cycle
**~80 min · 35 pts**

**Principle:** *A profitable company with a 90-day cycle can still die.*

**Why today:** Their degree covered working capital as a management topic. Diligence treats it as a valuation adjustment and a survival question, which is a different use of the same knowledge.

**Today's topics**
1. **The cash conversion cycle** — days inventory plus days receivable minus days payable. How long cash is tied up.
2. **Why growth consumes cash** — a growing business with a positive cycle needs more working capital every month.
3. **Normalised working capital** — the level a business needs to operate, which is a diligence output and a deal term.
4. **The working capital peg** — the mechanism in a transaction that adjusts price for the level delivered at closing.
5. **Seasonality** — a peg set at the wrong point in a cycle transfers value between buyer and seller.
6. **Manipulation before a sale** — stretching payables, accelerating collections, running down inventory. All visible in a monthly series.
7. **The Indian context** — long receivable cycles in B2B and government contracts, and what that does to a growing company.

**Read & watch**
- **A Big Four guide to working capital in transactions** · *Why this one: the peg mechanism explained by the people who negotiate it.*
- **A DRHP's working capital discussion** · *Why this one: Indian filings discuss working capital requirements explicitly, often with the funding need stated.*
- **An article on receivable cycles in Indian B2B or government contracting** · *Why this one: the India-specific reality that makes this module different from US material.*
- **Your module 4 day 26 runway material** · *Why this one: working capital consumption is a burn driver, and connecting the two is the point.*

**Today's challenge · ~35 min**
Calculate the cash conversion cycle for a company across twelve months of data. Identify seasonality and any month-end manipulation. Then calculate how much additional working capital 50% revenue growth would require, and state whether the company's cash position supports it.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why does a growing profitable company run out of cash?* → Growth increases receivables and inventory before the cash arrives. With a positive conversion cycle, every rupee of growth consumes cash — and profit on paper does not pay salaries.
2. *What is a working capital peg?* → An agreed normal level of working capital at closing. Delivering below it reduces the price; above it increases it. Without a peg, a seller can extract cash before closing by starving working capital.
3. *How do you detect pre-sale manipulation?* → Look at the monthly series rather than the year-end figure. Payables stretching, receivables collections accelerating and inventory running down in the final quarter is a recognisable pattern.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Analysing working capital only at year-end. The annual figure is the most manageable number in the accounts, and the monthly series is where the behaviour shows.

## Day 39 · Founder and team assessment
**~75 min · 30 pts**

**Principle:** *At seed stage you are underwriting the founder. Say so, and assess accordingly.*

**Why today:** The least quantitative day in the roadmap, and at early stage the most determinative. Finance people are frequently uncomfortable here and default to the numbers — which at seed is where the information is not.

**Today's topics**
1. **What you are actually assessing** — judgement, resilience, honesty about problems, ability to recruit, and founder-market fit.
2. **Founder-market fit** — why this person for this problem. Prior experience, unusual insight, or genuine obsession.
3. **Reference calls** — how to run them, who to speak to beyond the list provided, and what the off-list references reveal.
4. **The questions that surface judgement** — what has gone wrong, what you got wrong, what you would do differently.
5. **Team composition and gaps** — what is missing and whether the founder knows it.
6. **The equity split as a signal** — a founder split that does not reflect contribution predicts future conflict.
7. **Documenting a qualitative assessment** — writing it down so it can be checked later rather than remembered favourably.

**Read & watch**
- **YC Library — material on evaluating founders** · *Why this one: YC has the largest dataset on this question of anyone and publishes freely about it.*
- **First Round Review on reference checking** · *Why this one: the practical method, including back-channel references, which is where the real information is.*
- **A published VC's writing on founder assessment** · *Why this one: several partners write publicly about what they look for. Read two with different views.*
- **Your Thinking Under Uncertainty roadmap** · *Why this one: if the learner has done it, base rates and bias material applies directly here. Founder assessment is where analytical people most reliably fool themselves.*

**Today's challenge · ~25 min**
Write a founder assessment framework: six questions you would ask the founder, four you would ask a reference, and the three signals you would treat as serious concerns. Then write two paragraphs on how you would guard against your own bias — particularly liking someone.

**Check yourself**
1. *What does founder-market fit mean concretely?* → A specific reason this person is unusually well placed for this problem — prior operating experience in it, an insight from lived exposure, or a durable obsession. "Passionate about the space" is not a reason.
2. *Why do off-list references matter more?* → Provided references are selected. A former colleague found independently, or an investor from a prior company, gives you information the founder did not curate.
3. *What is the strongest positive signal in a founder conversation?* → Precise, unprompted honesty about what is not working. Founders who volunteer the weakest part of their business are almost always more reliable about everything else.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Substituting likeability for assessment. Founders who raise money are usually persuasive, and being persuaded is not the same as being convinced by evidence.

## Day 40 · Legal, regulatory and compliance red flags
**~80 min · 35 pts**

**Principle:** *The compliance history tells you how the company behaves when nobody is watching.*

**Why today:** You are not a lawyer, and you do not need to be. You need to know what to look for and when to escalate — which is a distinct and teachable skill.

**Today's topics**
1. **Corporate hygiene** — board resolutions, statutory registers, filings up to date. The cheapest signal of internal discipline available.
2. **Cap table integrity** — does the spreadsheet reconcile to MCA filings and the register of members.
3. **ESOP documentation** — grants made without board approval or scheme documentation are a common and expensive gap.
4. **Founder agreements** — vesting, IP assignment, non-compete. A departed co-founder holding unvested-in-practice equity is a live problem.
5. **IP ownership** — is the intellectual property assigned to the company or held personally by a founder or a contractor.
6. **Tax and statutory** — GST, TDS, PF and ESI compliance. Arrears here are both a liability and a signal.
7. **Sector licensing** — RBI for fintech, FSSAI for food, and other sector-specific requirements.
8. **When to escalate** — the point at which you stop and involve counsel.

**Read & watch**
- **An Indian law firm's startup diligence checklist** · *Why this one: the Indian document set specifically. US material will not cover MCA, GST, TDS or PF. Check the publication date.*
- **MCA filings for a real company** · *Why this one: check whether filings are current. Overdue filings are public, free to check, and immediately informative.*
- **A guide to IP assignment in Indian startups** · *Why this one: contractor-built IP without assignment is one of the most common material gaps.*
- **A DRHP's litigation and regulatory disclosures** · *Why this one: see what a company must disclose when a regulator reads it, and what those disclosures actually look like.*

**Today's challenge · ~35 min**
Take a real Indian startup. Check MCA for filing currency, directors and charges. Build a red-flag checklist across the seven categories above, marking what you can verify publicly, what requires document access, and what needs counsel. Then write the three items you would escalate first.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why do overdue MCA filings matter beyond the penalty?* → They indicate the company does not maintain basic hygiene. If the statutory filings are late, the option grants and board resolutions probably are too.
2. *What is the risk if IP is not assigned to the company?* → The company does not own its core asset. A contractor or departed founder may hold rights to the product, which is a deal-breaker until cured — and curing it requires their cooperation.
3. *When do you stop and involve counsel?* → Any unresolved cap table discrepancy, any IP ownership question, any founder dispute, and any regulatory arrears. Recognising the boundary of your competence is part of the professional skill.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Treating compliance as administrative. The compliance record is the highest-signal, lowest-cost proxy for how the company operates, and much of it is publicly checkable before you ask anyone a question.

## Day 41 · Writing the investment memo
**~90 min · 45 pts**

**Principle:** *A memo that does not state what would change your mind is not a memo.*

**Why today:** The module and the analytical arc of the roadmap end in a document. Everything from modules 3 through 6 exists to be written down here in a form someone else can decide from.

**Today's topics**
1. **What a memo is for** — enabling a decision by someone who has not done the work, and creating a record that can be checked later.
2. **Structure** — recommendation first, then thesis, market, team, business and unit economics, financials, valuation, risks, and what would change the view.
3. **Recommendation up front** — the reader should know your view in the first three sentences.
4. **The thesis** — what must be true for this to work, stated as falsifiable claims rather than description.
5. **Risks written honestly** — the ones you cannot resolve, not a list of generic startup risks.
6. **What would change your mind** — the section that separates a memo from a pitch, and the one most often omitted.
7. **Length and discipline** — two to four pages. A memo nobody finishes has failed regardless of quality.
8. **The appendix** — supporting analysis, with every figure traceable to a source.

**Read & watch**
- **Published investment memos** — several funds have released historical memos publicly · *Why this one: read two real ones before writing. The structure and the tone are learned by reading, not by description.*
- **A16z or First Round on investment decision-making** · *Why this one: how memos are actually used in a partnership, which shapes what belongs in one.*
- **Your Writing Clearly roadmap** — if built, otherwise the GOV.UK style guide · *Why this one: the memo is a writing task, and the constraint that improves it most is cutting length.*
- **Your module 3 day 20 and module 5 day 34 notes** · *Why this one: the valuation critique and the model audit both feed the memo. Reread what you wrote.*

**Today's challenge · ~45 min**
Write a complete investment memo on one real startup, three pages maximum. Include: recommendation, thesis as falsifiable claims, market with your bottom-up sizing, unit economics, valuation with your method stated, the three risks that matter, and an explicit section on what would change your view. Every figure traceable.

**Check yourself**
1. *Why does the recommendation come first?* → The reader is deciding whether to spend time, and a memo that withholds its conclusion wastes theirs. It also forces you to have a view rather than assembling material.
2. *What makes a thesis falsifiable?* → It states conditions that could be observed to be false. "The market is large" is not falsifiable; "SMB adoption will reach X because Y is now cheap enough" is.
3. *Why is "what would change my mind" the most important section?* → It converts an opinion into a testable position, tells the reader where the argument is weakest, and creates a record that makes you a better investor over time because you can check yourself later.

**The mistake almost everyone makes:** Writing the memo as advocacy. A memo that only supports the recommendation is a pitch, and readers discount it accordingly. The credibility comes from the risks section and the falsification conditions.

## Authoring notes

**Day 40 is the most India-specific day outside module 7** and needs the most verification. MCA, GST, TDS, PF and sector licensing have no US equivalent, and the free US diligence material will actively mislead. Use Indian law firm publications and check their dates.

**Day 39 is the day to write most carefully.** It is qualitative in a roadmap that is otherwise arithmetic, and it must not become a list of platitudes. The bias-control element — how you guard against liking someone — is what makes it rigorous rather than vague.

**Day 41 at 45 points is correctly weighted.** It is the longest challenge and it consolidates modules 3 through 6 into a single artefact.

**One module remains: 7, SME IPO and the Indian regulatory layer.** Seven days. It is the most India-specific, needs the most verification against current SEBI circulars and exchange criteria, and rots fastest — quarterly review, with `reviewed_at` shown prominently on the roadmap page.
